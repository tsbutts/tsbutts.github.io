Lithography

What it is

Lithography is a printing method based on the fact that oil and water don't mix. An artist draws an image on a flat surface, traditionally a smooth slab of limestone, using a greasy crayon or ink. The surface is then treated and dampened so that water clings only to the blank areas, and when oily ink is rolled over it, the ink sticks only to the drawing. Paper pressed against the surface picks up the image. Unlike woodblock printing, nothing is carved away, so the artist can draw with fine lines and soft shading.

A brief history

Lithography was invented in Germany around 1796 by Alois Senefelder, who was looking for a cheap way to print plays and music. It spread quickly across Europe, and artists such as Honoré Daumier used it to make satirical prints for newspapers. In the late 1800s, Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec and other artists in Paris used color lithography to create bold posters. Over time, metal plates replaced stone for most work, and a version called offset lithography became the main method for printing books, magazines and packaging. Artists still make hand-drawn lithographs today.

Source: The Metropolitan Museum of Art, Heilbrunn Timeline of Art History.

Quick facts

  • Material: a stone or metal plate, greasy ink and paper
  • Works because oil and water repel each other
  • Planographic: the printing surface stays flat
  • Allows detailed drawing and soft tones
