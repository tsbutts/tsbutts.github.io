Etching

What it is

Etching is a printmaking method in which acid is used to cut a design into a metal plate, usually copper or zinc. The artist first covers the plate with a waxy coating that resists acid, then scratches a drawing through the coating with a sharp needle. When the plate is placed in an acid bath, the acid bites into the metal only where the lines were drawn. The coating is removed, ink is pushed into the grooves, and the surface is wiped clean. Damp paper pressed against the plate in a heavy press pulls the ink out of the lines and onto the page. Because the artist draws with a needle, etching allows fine, flowing lines.

A brief history

Etching grew out of the work of armorers, who decorated metal armor with acid-cut designs. Artists in Europe began using the technique to make prints in the early 1500s. In the 1600s, Rembrandt van Rijn became famous for his etchings, using them to create detailed portraits, landscapes and scenes with rich contrasts of light and dark. Later artists, including Francisco Goya and Pablo Picasso, also made important etchings. Photographic and digital methods now handle much commercial printing, but artists still make etchings as fine art.

Source: The Metropolitan Museum of Art, Heilbrunn Timeline of Art History.

Quick facts

  • Material: a copper or zinc plate, acid, ink and paper
  • Intaglio method: ink sits in cut grooves, not on a raised surface
  • Acid cuts the lines, so no heavy carving is needed
  • Known for fine, detailed lines
